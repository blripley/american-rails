# Canadian Rails 1851 — board research

Research behind the Canadian map for *American Rails*: the Province of Canada
(Canada West and Canada East) plus New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward
Island, as they stood in **1851**.

Same convention as [board-research.md](board-research.md): every claim carries a
confidence level.

- **HIGH** — from a primary or well-established secondary source, quoted below.
- **MEDIUM** — consistent across sources but not verified against a census table.
- **LOW** — my estimate, used only to rank a town into a value band.

---

## Why 1851 is the right year

1851 is not an arbitrary date for a Canadian railway game — it is *the* date.

- The **Main Trunk Line of Railway Act** was passed by the Province of Canada in
  **1851**, committing the province to a trunk railway from its eastern to its
  western limit. The Grand Trunk was incorporated to build it the following year.
  (HIGH — Toronto Railway Historical Association; Britannica.)
- The **St Lawrence & Atlantic** agreed on **4 August 1851** to meet the Atlantic
  & St Lawrence at Island Pond, completing a line from Montréal to an ice-free
  winter port at Portland, Maine. Regular service began 4 April 1853. (HIGH —
  Wikipedia, *Canadian National Lines in New England*.)
- A **Board of Railway Commissioners** was formed in **1851** to administer the
  1849 Railway Guarantee Act. (HIGH.)
- **Major Robinson's** survey of the Chaleur Bay route for an intercolonial
  railway — Moncton north to Newcastle, Bathurst and Campbellton, then into
  Canada East — was recommended in **1849** and was the live proposal in 1851.
  (HIGH — *Intercolonial Railway*, Canadian Encyclopedia and Wikipedia.)
- The **St Andrews & Quebec Railway's** locomotive *Pioneer* landed at St Andrews
  in **March 1851**, before a strike, storm damage at Katy's Cove and political
  opposition stalled the line. (HIGH.)

So in 1851 every line on this map is either just built, under construction, or
being argued about in a legislature — exactly the position the American board
puts its players in.

**What actually existed in 1851** (HIGH): the Champlain & St Lawrence (1836, the
first railway in British North America), the Montreal & Lachine (1847), and the
**Albion Mines Railway** in Pictou County (opened 19 September 1839; 9.78 km from
Albion Mines to Dunbar's Point; locomotives *Samson*, *Hercules* and *John
Buddle* — "the first really effective steam operation in Canada"). Everything
else was survey stakes and prospectuses.

**Known gap.** The 1851 census of Canada West and Canada East was not enumerated
until **12 January 1852**, returns for several counties are missing, and the
original paper records were destroyed in 1955 after microfilming. For Nova
Scotia only Halifax and Kings County survive; for New Brunswick, Gloucester,
Kent, Queens and the **City of Saint John** are missing. Town-level figures below
the top tier are therefore MEDIUM at best.

## 1. Population — the value ladder

**Top tier (HIGH — Historical Atlas of Canada):**

| Place | 1851 population | Board value |
|---|---|---|
| Montréal | ~58,000 | 8/5 |
| Québec | ~42,000 | 7/5 |
| Toronto | ~30,000 (from under 2,000 in 1825) | 6/4 |
| Saint John, NB | ~22,700 | 5/3 |
| Halifax | ~20,700–29,000 (sources differ) | 5/3 |
| Hamilton | ~14,100 | 5/3 |
| Kingston | ~11,700 | 5/3 |
| Bytown (Ottawa) | ~7,800 | 5/3 |
| London | ~7,000 | 4/3 |

Note the ordering the sources are explicit about: *"Kingston (11,700) had fallen
behind Hamilton (14,100) and was being challenged by Ottawa (7,800) and London
(7,000)."*

**Second tier (MEDIUM).** The Historical Atlas describes Guelph, St Catharines,
Brantford and Belleville as a distinct second tier of manufacturing towns. Their
individual 1851 figures are not recoverable from the surviving returns, so their
values are set by economic function — canal, coalfield, capital, timber port —
rather than by a headcount.

**A caution.** An early search returned "Montreal 140,747 / Toronto 86,415" for
1851. Those are later-century figures and are wrong for this period; the numbers
above are the ones used.

## 2. Terrain — two barriers, not one

This is the single biggest design difference from the American board, and it is
straight geography rather than invention.

The American map has **one** barrier, the Appalachian spine. Eastern Canada has
**two**, with one narrow fertile corridor between them:

| Board terrain | Canadian region | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Mountain** ($5, one cube) | **Canadian Shield** edge, **Laurentians**, **Notre-Dame Mountains**, **Sutton Mountains**, **New Brunswick Highlands**, **Cobequid Hills**, **Cape Breton Highlands** | Natural Resources Canada physiographic regions |
| **Forest** ($3, one cube) | Shield interior, Ottawa Valley pineries, Eastern Townships, Acadian interior | The Shield is boreal forest on rock — timber country, not alpine |
| **Plains** ($2 + $2, multi-cube) | **St Lawrence Lowlands**, the Ontario peninsula, the Saint John valley, the Annapolis Valley, the Maritime Plain | The Great Lakes–St Lawrence Lowlands region |

The 1851 timing matters here too: *"the agricultural settlement of the Great
Western Peninsula ran up against the Canadian Shield"* in exactly this period,
just as the last corners toward Georgian Bay and up the Ottawa were being
surveyed. The Shield is the northern wall of the settled world in 1851, and the
government began the Ottawa–Huron Tract colonisation scheme that same decade.

### Three chokepoints that are real places

1. **The Frontenac Axis** — the Shield reaches south to the St Lawrence near
   Kingston. On the board, two mountain tiles at (6,8) and (7,8) pinch the
   corridor.
2. **The Matapédia gap** — the only land route from Canada East into New
   Brunswick, over the Notre-Dame range to Chaleur Bay. This is precisely the
   route Robinson surveyed in 1848–49 and the reason the Intercolonial argument
   ran for twenty years.
3. **The Chignecto Isthmus** — 24 km of land between The Bend and Amherst, the
   only land connection into Nova Scotia.

## 3. Hubs and connection cities

**Five non-developable port hubs:** Montréal, Québec, Toronto, Halifax, Saint
John. These were the five great ports of British North America in 1851 —
Montréal at the head of ocean navigation (dredging of the St Lawrence began in
1850), Québec the timber and shipbuilding port, Halifax the ice-free Atlantic
port and Royal Navy station, Saint John the timber and shipbuilding capital of
New Brunswick.

**Three special-connection cities: Toronto — Montréal — Halifax.** This is the
Main Trunk Line of 1851 joined to the Intercolonial dream attached to it, and it
maps onto the American board's Chicago–New York–Atlanta triangle without
straining: a western terminus, the commercial centre, and the ocean port.

**The year track is unchanged at 1851–1857**, and every year in it lands on
something real: the Act in 1851, the Grand Trunk incorporated 1852, the Great
Western opened 1853, the Victoria Bridge begun 1854.

## 4. Period names — and four traps

Names are as used **in 1851**, not as they are today:

| On the board | Today | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Bytown** | Ottawa | Renamed 1855 |
| **The Bend** | Moncton | "The Bend of Petitcodiac"; renamed 1855 |
| **Port Sarnia** | Sarnia | "Sarnia" from 1856 |
| **Newcastle** | Miramichi | Amalgamated only in 1995 |
| **Berlin** | Kitchener | Renamed 1916 (not on the board, but noted) |

Places deliberately **excluded** as not yet existing: **Collingwood** (named
1853; "Hen and Chickens Harbour" in 1851 — **Barrie** is used instead), and
**Fort William**, which sits west of the map's left edge.

### The name-collision trap (engineering, not history)

The engine looks cities up **by name** (`cityHexByName`), so names must be
unique. 1851 Canada collides with itself four times:

- **Chatham** — Canada West *and* New Brunswick → the Canada West town is used;
  the Miramichi town is named **Newcastle**.
- **Windsor** — Ontario *and* Nova Scotia → Ontario only.
- **Woodstock** — Canada West *and* New Brunswick → neither is on the board.
- **Saint John** (NB) vs **Saint-Jean** (Canada East) → Saint-Jean is omitted.

`build-map.mjs` fails the build if a duplicate name ever reappears.

## 5. Departures from true geography

The map is a projection of real coordinates, not a drawing. Longitude and
latitude are converted to hex columns and rows by `scripts/canada/project.mjs`
from the real coordinates in `cities.json`. Two deliberate distortions:

**Latitude is stretched ~25%.** True isotropic scale is 1.392 rows per degree of
latitude; the map uses **1.75**. The land band is far wider than it is tall, so
there was vertical room to spend, and spending it pulls the crowded Lake Ontario
shore apart into separate tiles.

**Thirteen cities are nudged one tile.** Southern Ontario and the St Lawrence
corridor pack towns closer together than one hex (~92 km), so where two cities
landed on one tile, one is moved to the adjacent tile that preserves their real
compass relationship. Every nudge is recorded in `cities.json` as `nudge` with a
`nudgeWhy` explaining it — for example Buffalo *"south of St. Catharines across
the Niagara River"*, Amherst *"south-east of The Bend across the Chignecto
Isthmus"*.

**Two towns were cut** for density, not history: **Guelph** (~1,860) and
**Goderich** (~1,300). The Ontario peninsula is only about twenty tiles wide and
was carrying thirteen cities — far denser than anywhere on the American board.

**Two towns were added** to give the north a purpose: **Pembroke** (Ottawa Valley
timber, settled 1828) and **Owen Sound** (Georgian Bay harbour). Without them the
Shield is fifty tiles of scenery no player would ever enter.

## 6. Balance against the American board

| | American | Canadian |
|---|---|---|
| Tiles | 215 | 165 |
| Cities | 39 | 45 |
| **Total full city value** | **128** | **127** |
| Total shared value | 78 | 81 |
| Plains | 62 | 31 |
| Forest | 94 | 54 |
| Mountain | 20 | 35 |

Total city value — the anchor for the bot's measured economics (income 6→22,
$3.13 per cube, ~13 cubes per company, ~62% share sell-through) — lands within
one point of the American board without being tuned to.

**The two figures to watch in playtesting** are tiles (165 vs 215) and plains
(31 vs 62). The board is smaller and harder to cross, so routes are shorter and
more contested, and fewer tiles can hold two companies' track. That is
thematically correct for a country with two mountain barriers, but it is a real
shift and needs measuring with bot games rather than assuming.
