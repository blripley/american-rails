# Western Canada Board (CPR, 1881-87) — board research

Research behind the third *American Rails* map: the Canadian Pacific Railway's
transcontinental build across Manitoba, Saskatchewan (then part of the
North-West Territories), Alberta (also North-West Territories) and British
Columbia, as they stood in **1881-1887**.

Same convention as [canada-board-research.md](canada-board-research.md) and
[board-research.md](board-research.md): every substantive claim carries a
confidence level.

- **HIGH** — from a primary source (an Act, a census, a national historic
  site designation) or a well-established secondary source (Canadian
  Encyclopedia, a named scholarly/archival source), quoted below.
- **MEDIUM** — consistent across sources but not verified against a primary
  document, or a specific figure from a single reputable secondary source
  I could not cross-check.
- **LOW** — thin, conflicting, or estimated; used only to flag a candidate
  or rank it into a value band, never to assert a fact as settled.

This design deliberately fictionalizes a real near-monopoly into six
companies (§3), and the two remaining hub cities are an open research
question this document is meant to close (§2) — both are flagged clearly
where the sourcing runs out.

## Why 1881-1887 is the right window

- **1871 — the ten-year promise.** British Columbia's Terms of Union
  (Article 11) committed the Dominion "to secure the commencement
  simultaneously, within two years from the date of the Union, of the
  construction of a Railway from the Pacific towards the Rocky Mountains,
  and from such point as may be selected, East of the Rocky Mountains,
  towards the Pacific, to connect the seaboard of British Columbia with the
  railway system of Canada; and further, to secure the completion of such
  Railway within ten years from the date of the Union." BC became Canada's
  sixth province on **20 July 1871**. (HIGH — *British Columbia Terms of
  Union*, 1871, Article 11; Legislative Assembly of BC.) The ten-year clock
  this sets expires in 1881, which is exactly when the private syndicate
  contract that actually built the railway was signed — the promise and the
  real start of construction line up almost to the year.
- **1872-1880 — false starts.** An earlier, government-built approach under
  contractor Andrew Onderdonk and a first syndicate (Sir Hugh Allan's, which
  collapsed in the 1873 Pacific Scandal) delayed things so badly that BC
  threatened in 1874 to secede, and Premier Walkem petitioned Queen Victoria
  directly for relief. (HIGH — Legislative Assembly of BC; *British Columbia
  and Confederation*, Canadian Encyclopedia.) This is useful background for
  *why* the game doesn't start in 1871: nothing real was under construction
  yet.
- **1880-81 — the Syndicate contract.** The Canadian Pacific Syndicate
  agreement was signed **21 October 1880**; the enabling Act received royal
  assent **15 February 1881**, and the Canadian Pacific Railway Company was
  incorporated the next day, **16 February 1881**. The five named syndicate
  members were George Stephen, Duncan McIntyre, Richard B. Angus, James J.
  Hill and John Stewart Kennedy (Donald Smith was a silent, unnamed partner
  at first). Terms: $25 million in cash subsidy, a 25-million-acre land
  grant, and duty-free import of construction material, in exchange for
  building the remaining ~1,900 km of main line within **ten years**. (HIGH
  — *An Act Respecting the Canadian Pacific Railway*, 1881; Encyclopedia.com
  company history; multiple secondary sources agree on the Feb 1881 dates.)
  **1881 is the correct start year**: it is the year the company legally
  exists and the year real construction west of Winnipeg begins in earnest.
- **1882 — the prairies.** William Cornelius Van Horne was hired as general
  manager in December 1881/early 1882 and promised (and nearly delivered)
  500 miles of main line in a single season; the American contracting firm
  Langdon, Shepard and Company built roughly 417-673 km that year alone,
  reaching close to Regina by year's end. (HIGH for the hire and the
  contract; MEDIUM for the exact mileage, which several sources state
  slightly differently — 500 mi promised vs. ~417-673 km actually laid.)
- **1883 — Calgary and the mountains.** The line reached Calgary in August
  1883, and by year's end had reached the Rocky Mountains' eastern
  foothills; the "prairie section" was declared complete 18 August 1883.
  Major A.B. Rogers found the pass through the Selkirk Mountains that bears
  his name in 1881-82. (HIGH.)
- **1884-85 — the mountains and the last gap.** The government's Railway
  Relief Bill (31 January 1884) provided a further $22.5 million loan that
  kept construction going through the Rockies and Selkirks — the hardest and
  most expensive terrain on the whole line. The Lake Superior section (the
  other famously difficult stretch, built through Canadian Shield rock
  since 1875) was finished 16 May 1885. (HIGH.)
- **7 November 1885 — Craigellachie.** Donald Smith drove the ceremonial
  last spike at Craigellachie, BC, near Eagle Pass, at 9:22 am, six years
  ahead of the ten-year deadline set in 1871. Craigellachie was designated a
  National Historic Site in 1971. (HIGH — Canadian Encyclopedia, *The Last
  Spike*; Parks Canada.) **This is the fixed, unambiguous end-of-story date**
  the board's era is built around.
- **1886 — regular transcontinental service.** The first regularly
  scheduled through passenger train, the *Pacific Express*, left Montréal
  on 28 June 1886 and reached Port Moody, BC (the terminus at the time) on
  4 July 1886. Vancouver was incorporated 6 April 1886 after being chosen
  over Port Moody as the permanent Pacific terminus; the first
  transcontinental train reached Vancouver itself on 23 May 1887. (HIGH —
  Legislative Assembly of BC; multiple secondary sources agree on both
  dates.) **This is why the board's year track should run a couple of years
  past the Last Spike** rather than stopping cold in 1885: the payoff of a
  completed railway — through service, the terminus fight, Vancouver's
  founding — all happens in 1886-87.

**Conclusion: 1881 (Syndicate incorporated) to 1887 (Vancouver receiving its
first through train; the Rocky Mountains Park Act formalizing Banff) is not
an arbitrary seven-year window — it is the real span of the story, bookended
by two dates with primary-source citations on both ends.**

**Known gap.** No comprehensive 1881-87 town-by-town population table exists
the way the Historical Atlas of Canada gave the 1851 Canadian board a clean
value ladder. The 1881 Census of Canada covered only Manitoba and the small
established parts of the NWT; most of the towns below did not exist yet when
it was taken and are documented instead by railway/land-boom histories,
which report population as a range, a "few hundred," or a snapshot at
incorporation years later. **Do not expect a Historical-Atlas-quality
population ladder for this board** — the honest state of the evidence is a
mix of a few hard census numbers (Winnipeg, Manitoba, Victoria) and a much
larger set of qualitative "boomtown / tent town / divisional point"
descriptions. This is itself period-accurate: most of this map was settled
*during* the seven years the board covers.

## 1. Real cities, circa 1881-1887

Organized by present-day province/territory, since that's how the design
groups them (Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta, BC). Historically, everything
outside Manitoba was the unified North-West Territories until 1905 — Regina
was NWT's capital, not a provincial one, and "Alberta" and "Saskatchewan"
were administrative districts, not provinces, in this era. Populations are
tagged with the era they're drawn from, not modern figures.

## 2. Hub-city recommendation

Winnipeg, Calgary and Vancouver are settled (design doc, confirmed
2026-08-23). The question is which **two** of Edmonton, Regina, and Victoria
— or an alternative — should round out the five non-developable hubs.

**Recommendation: Victoria and Regina.**

| Candidate | Real 1881-87 significance | On the CPR main line? | Population |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Victoria** | Provincial capital throughout the entire era (capital of the colony since 1866, of the province since 1871); BC's unchallenged metropolis until 1886. The 1871 rail promise was quite literally made *to* Victoria — Premier Walkem's 1874 petition over the broken promise was framed around delivering a railway "to Victoria." Home to the Royal Navy's Pacific headquarters at adjoining Esquimalt. | No — Vancouver Island, reached only by the separate Esquimalt & Nanaimo Railway (not part of the CPR system in this era; see §3) and by sea. | **5,925** at the 1881 census — the largest hard figure of any candidate in this section, HIGH confidence. |
| **Regina** | NWT territorial capital from 1883; NWMP headquarters from 1882; a CPR-selected townsite in the most literal sense — the company and government together chose its exact location. The seat of government for the entire region this board covers (everything except Manitoba and BC). | Yes — directly on the main line. | No hard era figure found; inferred low hundreds to low thousands (2,249 at the 1901 census is the first solid number). |
| **Edmonton** | A real, continuously occupied fur-trade settlement, but **explicitly bypassed** by the CPR mainline in favour of the Calgary/Kicking Horse Pass route; not reached by any railway until 1891 (after this board's year range). Growth "tailed off" once the bypass became clear. | No — and unlike Victoria, it isn't even the provincial capital of anything in this era, nor a major population or naval centre. | No hard era figure found; described only qualitatively as small. |

**Reasoning.** Regina is the easiest call: it is the seat of territorial
government and police power for the entire non-Manitoba, non-BC portion of
the map, and it sits directly on the corridor the game already treats as
the spine of the board. Making it non-developable captures the fact that,
historically, Regina's existence and location were dictated by the railway
company itself rather than the other way around — an inversion of the
American board's organically-grown cities that's worth preserving as a hub
rather than a developable stop.

Victoria is the harder call, precisely because it is *not* on the CPR main
line — exactly the same objection that rules out Edmonton. The
Halifax/Saint John precedent from the Canadian 1851 board is directly on
point: that board already makes non-mainline great ports into
non-developable hubs, because their real economic and political weight
justified it even though the trunk railway ran nowhere near them (Halifax
is 1,240 km from Montréal over a mountain range, per that document). Victoria
is the same shape of exception: the *biggest, best-documented population
figure of any candidate researched here* (5,925 in 1881, versus no verified
hard number for Regina or Edmonton), the actual provincial capital for the
entire era, and the place the original 1871 promise was made to satisfy.
Excluding it because it's on an island would be applying a rule the
existing boards don't apply to themselves.

Edmonton loses on every axis that matters for hub status: not on the
mainline, not a capital of anything in this era, no verified population
figure, and its defining historical fact for 1881-87 is precisely that it
*missed out* on the railway. That's a genuinely good story to tell on the
map (Edmonton absolutely belongs as a city — the design already scopes it
in explicitly, and its "the railway was supposed to come here" backstory is
excellent flavour), but it argues against hub status, not for it. A
developable Edmonton city hex, reachable only by a spur off the main
prairie corridor mirroring the real Calgary-Edmonton branch (even though
that branch is technically post-1887), would honor the history better than
forcing it into a non-developable slot it never earned in this window.

**If a third alternative is wanted:** no better-justified candidate turned
up in this research. Prince Albert (capital of the District of
Saskatchewan) is the next most plausible administrative-capital argument,
but it is off the main line even more decisively than Edmonton (reachable
historically only via the Qu'Appelle, Long Lake and Saskatchewan Railroad,
which wasn't finished until 1889) and has a smaller, less-documented
population. It's a good developable city, not a hub.

## 3. CPR subsidiary/division naming — the hard part

The design wants one dominant "CPR Mainline" plus five subsidiaries with
real, period-appropriate names, the same way the Canadian board uses Grand
Trunk, Great Western, Northern, etc. **Honest assessment up front: there are
not five genuinely separate, independently-operating regional railway
companies in Western Canada in 1881-87 the way there were competing lines in
1850s Ontario.** The CPR's near-monopoly here is real, not a gap in this
research. But there is more real material to work with than "the CPR
built everything" — it breaks down into a few different categories, of
different strength:

### Real, independently-chartered colonization railways (strongest category)

These are genuine companies, with their own charters and shareholders, that
operated in exactly this region and era under the federal "colonization
railway" land-subsidy policy — built specifically to open up prairie land
the CPR main line missed, and in every case eventually absorbed by or
leased to the CPR (some quite quickly, some decades later):

- **Manitoba and North Western Railway** (originally chartered as the
  "Portage, Westbourne and North Western") — construction started 1881,
  reached Gladstone by 1882, financed initially by Morgan Grenfell/J.P.
  Morgan's London affiliate, later controlled by shipping magnate Hugh
  Allan's family. Ran from Portage la Prairie toward Yorkton. (HIGH —
  Manitoba Historical Society, SaskToday.)
- **Manitoba South Western Colonization Railway** — grading underway 1881
  between Winnipeg and Gretna and toward Manitou; its Pembina Mountain
  Branch was leased to the CPR "in perpetuity" almost immediately, and the
  company was CPR-controlled from the late 1880s (fully absorbed only in
  1957, but functionally a CPR creature from very early on). (HIGH.)
- **Qu'Appelle, Long Lake and Saskatchewan Railroad and Steamboat
  Company** — chartered and construction begun 1883 to run Regina toward
  Prince Albert via Saskatoon; ran into financial trouble (only 25 miles
  built by 1886) and was eventually bought outright by the CPR, which used
  it as its northward branch into Saskatchewan. Directly connects to the
  Regina/Prince Albert pairing noted in §1b. (HIGH.)

These three are the strongest raw material for subsidiary names: real
companies, chartered and building track in exactly this window, all
eventually CPR-controlled — which is precisely the "fictionalize the
monopoly into subsidiaries" story the design wants, because historically
they *were* absorbed subsidiaries of the same monopoly, just a few years
after this board's window closes for two of them.

### A real BC railway that fits the theme but has a genuine date problem

- **Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway (E&N)** — incorporated 27 September 1883
  by coal baron Robert Dunsmuir, built 1884-86 (last spike driven by Sir
  John A. Macdonald himself, 13 August 1886) under a federal land grant
  tied to the same BC-Confederation rail promise this whole board is about.
  **The catch: the E&N was independently owned by the Dunsmuir family and
  was not sold to the CPR until 1905** — nearly two decades after this
  board's year range ends. Using it as a "CPR subsidiary" for an 1881-87
  board is anachronistic if taken literally. It is, however, exactly the
  kind of real, regional, period-correct railway name the design is
  looking for, and the fictionalization the design has already approved
  (folding a real monopoly into competing "divisions") is arguably no
  bigger a liberty than back-dating this one company's ownership by twenty
  years — flagging it clearly rather than either silently using it or
  silently dropping it.

### Real names that are contractors and construction sections, not companies

- **Andrew Onderdonk** held the actual contracts (numbered individually,
  not under a fancy trade name) for the Fraser Canyon/mountain section from
  Yale to Savona's Ferry and on to Port Moody and Eagle Pass — roughly 341
  km of the hardest terrain on the whole line, 1880-85. (HIGH.)
- **Langdon, Shepard and Company**, a Minnesota-based contracting firm, held
  the main prairie-section contract in 1882, building the bulk of the track
  from Winnipeg's area toward Calgary under Van Horne's direction. (HIGH.)
- The **Lake Superior section** was built by the government (from 1875) and
  then the CPR itself through a patchwork of individual contractors (R.G.
  Reid, Kenneth McLeod, and others named in the sources), not one single
  named company. (MEDIUM — individual contractor names are sourced, but no
  single umbrella company name covers the whole section.)
- This research did **not** find primary-source evidence that the CPR used
  formally chartered, trademarked division names like "Prairie Division" or
  "Mountain Division" as period (1880s) terminology — those exist as
  informal, functional descriptions in modern secondary sources (this
  document uses them the same way) and as later, genuinely-official CPR
  operating division names, but I could not confirm they were used as such
  during initial construction. **Do not present "CPR Mountain Division" as
  a period-authentic proper name at HIGH confidence** — the honest and
  well-documented period reality is a *place* name (a divisional point like
  Donald, BC, or Medicine Hat) or a *contractor's* name (Onderdonk,
  Langdon & Shepard), not a company-style division brand.
- The **Canada Central Railway** was a real, independently-chartered
  railway (Ottawa/Brockville area) leased and absorbed into the CPR in June
  1881 — the company's very first acquisition. It's real and period-correct,
  but it's an Ontario/eastern-approach company, geographically outside this
  board's Manitoba-Saskatchewan-Alberta-BC scope, so it fits the *history*
  of "CPR absorbing real companies" better than it fits *this specific
  map's* geography.
- **St. Paul, Minneapolis and Manitoba Railway** (James J. Hill's company,
  reorganized from the St. Paul & Pacific in 1879) — genuinely real, built
  the Pembina Branch that gave Winnipeg its first rail connection in 1878,
  and Hill was literally one of the five named 1881 CPR Syndicate members
  before splitting off in 1883 to build the Great Northern instead. It is
  an American company operating a branch into Manitoba, not a Western
  Canadian regional line, and its most senior figure left the CPR syndicate
  during this exact era — a good story element, weaker as a clean
  "subsidiary" name.

### Honest verdict for the design

There are **three solid, unambiguous, period-correct chartered-company
names** available (Manitoba and North Western; Manitoba South Western
Colonization; Qu'Appelle, Long Lake and Saskatchewan) that were real
companies, in the real region, in the real era, all eventually folded into
the CPR system — this is as close to a perfect match for "give the
monopoly's pieces real absorbed-company names" as the historical record
offers. A fourth (Esquimalt and Nanaimo) is real and thematically perfect
but requires either accepting a ~20-year ownership anachronism or being
explicit that it represents "the parallel, non-CPR promise-fulfilling
railway of the same era" rather than a literal CPR subsidiary. For the
fifth slot (and optionally to avoid the E&N anachronism entirely), the
strongest option is **not** inventing a fictional company name, but naming
a division after a real, sourced contractor or section — e.g., something
built around **Onderdonk** (the mountain/Fraser Canyon contract) or
**Langdon & Shepard** (the prairie contract) — which keeps every name
tracing to a real, checkable historical entity even though it changes the
naming convention from "company" to "contractor." **My recommendation: use
the three colonization railways plus Onderdonk's contract and Langdon &
Shepard's contract for the five subsidiaries, and treat Esquimalt &
Nanaimo as a tempting but rejected sixth option** rather than stretch the
"company" framing to cover a contractor relationship the same way the
existing boards' "Grand Trunk"/"Great Western" names cover actual
railway corporations. This is a case where the design will get more real
history by accepting two "contractor" names among the five than by trying
to force five chartered-company names to exist where the record only
clearly supports three.

## 4. Terrain and geography

Sourced primarily from Natural Resources Canada's *Physiographic Regions of
Canada* framework (atlas.gc.ca/phys/en), the same authority the Canadian
1851 board cites for its terrain (HIGH for the framework itself; MEDIUM for
some of the connecting narrative below, which draws on Parks Canada and
Britannica for the named passes).

- **Interior Plains.** Covers all of Manitoba and Saskatchewan and most of
  Alberta in this map's scope — flat to gently rolling prairie, exactly the
  terrain that let Van Horne's crews lay 500+ miles in a single season
  (1882). This is the map's "Plains" terrain almost everywhere east of
  Calgary.
- **The foothills transition.** Around Calgary and Cochrane, the Interior
  Plains meet the Cordillera abruptly — the front ranges of the Rocky
  Mountains rise with comparatively little intermediate zone. This is where
  Plains terrain should give way to Mountain terrain on the board.
- **The Rocky Mountains and Kicking Horse Pass.** The CPR's 1881 decision to
  route through Kicking Horse Pass (rather than the previously-assumed
  Yellowhead Pass near Edmonton) is the single routing decision that made
  Calgary a rail town and left Edmonton bypassed (see §1c, §2). The pass
  itself, on the Continental Divide, is notorious for the steep "Big Hill"
  grade down to Field — later eased by the Spiral Tunnels (built well after
  this board's era, so not relevant to period geography, only to modern
  visitors).
- **The Rocky Mountain Trench** separates the Rocky Mountains proper from
  the **Columbia Mountains** (a group that includes the Selkirk, Purcell,
  Monashee and Cariboo ranges) — Golden sits in this trench along the
  Columbia River, between the two mountain systems.
- **Rogers Pass**, through the **Selkirk Mountains** (part of the Columbia
  Mountains, not the Rockies), was found by A.B. Rogers in 1881-82 and
  was, by every contemporary account, the single hardest and most
  dangerous stretch of the whole railway — the last major barrier before
  the descent toward Revelstoke and the Eagle Pass/Craigellachie area where
  the two construction crews finally met.
- **The Interior Plateau** of British Columbia lies between the Columbia
  Mountains (east) and the Coast Mountains (west) — a drier, rolling
  plateau country where Kamloops sits, along the Thompson River.
- **The Fraser Canyon** is the dramatic gorge Onderdonk's contract blasted
  and tunnelled through, connecting the Interior Plateau down to the lower
  Fraser Valley — the most expensive, most labour-intensive stretch of the
  entire BC section (the ~7,000-worker figure at Yale reflects this).
- **The Lower Mainland / Fraser Valley** is flat lowland delta country
  where New Westminster, Port Moody and Vancouver/Granville all sit,
  opening onto the Strait of Georgia.
- **Vancouver Island**, across the Strait of Georgia, is physically
  disconnected from the mainline entirely — home to Victoria, Esquimalt and
  Nanaimo, served in this era only by sea and (from 1886) the separate
  Esquimalt & Nanaimo Railway. This physical separation is the geographic
  fact underpinning the whole Victoria-as-hub argument in §2.
- **A northern boreal-forest belt** lies north of the settled prairie band
  in Saskatchewan/Manitoba (the transition toward the Canadian Shield and
  the sub-Arctic) and in BC's northern Interior Plateau — matching the
  design brief's "boreal forest belt in the north" note. This band saw very
  little of the actual 1881-87 CPR story (which stayed on the southern,
  prairie/mountain corridor) and should probably read on the board as
  sparse, low-value scenery rather than a second developed corridor — the
  same design choice the Canadian board made with its Shield interior.

## 5. Period-name traps — a checklist for the build pass

Matching the Canadian board doc's "period names and traps" section, these
are the places where using the modern name would be flatly wrong for part
or all of 1881-87:

| On the board (if using strict period naming) | Modern name | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Granville** (or "Gastown" informally) | Vancouver | Not renamed until incorporation, 6 April 1886 — wrong for roughly the first five of the board's seven years. The design has already committed to "Vancouver" as a connection/hub city name; this is flagged as a known, accepted anachronism, not a suggestion to relitigate it. |
| **Coalbanks** | Lethbridge | Renamed October 1885 — "Lethbridge" is only accurate for the last ~2 years of the board's window. |
| **Fort Calgary** vs. **Calgary** | Calgary | Named "Calgary" from 1876, well before this board's window opens — no trap here, "Calgary" is correct throughout. |
| **Troy** | Qu'Appelle (Station) | Renamed 1884 when the CPR arrived; "Troy" is correct 1882-84, "Qu'Appelle Station" 1884-87. |

## 6. Gaps and open items for the build pass

- **Population figures are genuinely thin for most prairie/BC towns.**
  Unlike the 1851 Canadian board's Historical Atlas value ladder, this era
  has to be priced mostly from qualitative role (divisional point vs.
  branch terminus vs. territorial capital vs. construction camp) rather
  than a clean population number. Expect city income values to be set by
  narrative importance more than by a defensible headcount, and say so in
  the eventual city-value notes the way `cities.json` already does for the
  Canadian board's second tier.
- **Rat Portage/Kenora** is a real, well-documented, extremely on-theme
  place (a literal Manitoba-vs-Ontario jurisdictional fight over CPR track)
  that sits right at the edge of the stated four-territory scope. Recommend
  a build-phase decision rather than resolving it here.
- **Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway's** twenty-year ownership gap (independent
  until 1905, not a CPR subsidiary in 1881-87) is the sharpest naming
  compromise in §3 — worth a deliberate sign-off from whoever owns the
  company-naming decision, rather than sliding it in silently.
- A handful of prairie/BC-interior names (Virden, Gladstone, Minnedosa;
  Lytton, Ashcroft, Spences Bridge) came up during this research without
  citable era-specific detail. If the eventual city count needs more
  density than the 37 candidates above provide, these are the names worth
  a dedicated follow-up search rather than inventing populations for towns
  already on this list.
- **Craigellachie** needs a design decision: it is the single most
  important place-name in the entire story and essentially uninhabited —
  it may fit the board better as a marked terrain feature/route waypoint
  near Eagle Pass than as a priced city hex.

## 7. Addendum (board-rework-2): Vancouver Island removed, northern corridor densified

Ben played the board himself and gave direct feedback: the Vancouver Island
water-gap crossing "does not fit on the board very well" and should be
removed, and the northern Edmonton-Battleford-Prince Albert branch has "no
benefit to building up that direction right now" and should get more towns.
This addendum documents both changes' research.

### 7a. Vancouver Island removal

Victoria, Esquimalt and Nanaimo (§1d above) were removed from the board
entirely, along with the hand-carved water-gap terrain and the two grid
rows that existed only to give the island room south of the mainland. This
is a board-content decision, not a correction to the research above — §1d's
entries for these three places remain accurate historical research, just no
longer represented on the map. It does not touch the hub set: Victoria was
never one of the 5 locked hubs (§2's own recommendation to make Victoria a
hub was already overridden by the locked design decision before this
rework), and New Westminster and Port Moody — real mainland BC coast
cities, never part of the island problem — are unaffected and remain on the
board.

### 7b. Four new northern towns, researched to the same HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW standard as §1

Candidates considered: Fort Carlton, Fort Pitt, Duck Lake, Batoche, Victoria
Mission/Pakan, and St. Albert. All six are real, documented 1861-87
settlements in or near this corridor. Four were selected for real,
well-sourced geographic spread along the route (rather than piling three or
four onto the same tight Duck Lake/Batoche/Fort Carlton cluster, which sit
within ~30km of each other in reality); Duck Lake and St. Albert were set
aside as the two thinner/most-redundant candidates, not because either is
poorly documented, but see the notes below.

| Place | Name in use 1881-87 | Notes | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fort Pitt** | Fort Pitt | HBC fur-trade/provisioning post on the North Saskatchewan River, roughly midway between Edmonton and Battleford — real, on the Carlton Trail corridor. A 1885 North-West Rebellion site: besieged and burned by Cree forces under Big Bear in April 1885 (in the aftermath of the Frog Lake killings); NWMP/HBC occupants evacuated downriver to Battleford. No town population figure exists — it was a fur-trade post/garrison, not a settled town, the same honest limitation Fort Macleod and Fort Qu'Appelle already carry on this board. | HIGH (role, dates, Rebellion involvement — Canadian Encyclopedia, Parks Canada); no population claim made |
| **Fort Carlton** | Fort Carlton | HBC fur-trade/provisioning post on the North Saskatchewan River near Duck Lake, established 1810 — one of the HBC's most important prairie posts. Served as the main North-West Mounted Police base of operations in the district through most of this board's window, until the Battle of Duck Lake (26 March 1885) forced its evacuation; it was accidentally burned shortly after. Real and heavily documented as a 1885 Rebellion site (the Métis provisional government at Batoche demanded its surrender in March 1885). Like Fort Pitt, no town population figure exists — a fort/post, not a settled town. | HIGH (role, dates, Rebellion involvement — Canadian Encyclopedia, Parks Canada, University of Saskatchewan's Indigenous Saskatchewan Encyclopedia); no population claim made |
| **Batoche** | Batoche | Métis settlement founded 1872 by colonists from the Red River Settlement; Xavier Letendre "dit Batoche" built a ferry crossing there in 1873. Became the seat of Louis Riel's provisional government (declared 18 March 1885) and the site of the climactic Battle of Batoche (9-12 May 1885), the resistance's final and most significant engagement. Population **500** in 1885 — a real, cross-referenced hard figure, better-documented than most of this board's existing thin/unsourced places. | HIGH (settlement history, population figure, Rebellion role — Wikipedia/Parks Canada's Batoche National Historic Site page, Britannica, University of Saskatchewan) |
| **Victoria Settlement** | Victoria Settlement (also Fort Victoria for the adjoining HBC post; "Pakan," after Cree chief Pakannuk, became the common name later) | Methodist mission founded 1862 by Rev. George McDougall on the North Saskatchewan River east of Edmonton, on a traditional Indigenous camping ground; the HBC's own adjoining post, Fort Victoria, dates to 1864. Métis population grew to roughly 130 by 1870 as Red River families took up river-lot farms; the mission ran one of the first Protestant schools west of Manitoba and later a hospital. No population figure specific to 1881-87 itself was found — the 1870 figure is the best-documented number and is not extrapolated forward. | HIGH (founding, role — Canadian Encyclopedia, Alberta's Historic Places, OMI World); MEDIUM (population, era-specific) |

**Why Duck Lake and St. Albert were set aside:**

- **Duck Lake** — real and extremely well-documented (site of the opening
  Battle of Duck Lake, 26 March 1885, the conflict's first engagement) but
  sits only ~10-20km from both Fort Carlton and Batoche in reality. At this
  board's hex scale, all three compress toward the same 1-2 hexes; adding
  Duck Lake alongside both would have meant either an implausible pile of
  nudges in one small corner of the board or effectively duplicating
  Fort Carlton/Batoche's own story beats (the same 1885 Rebellion opening
  chapter) without adding new geographic reach. Fort Carlton and Batoche
  between them already carry Duck Lake's historical moment (the Battle of
  Duck Lake was fought between the two).
- **St. Albert** — real, well-documented (founded 1861 by Father Albert
  Lacombe, Alberta's oldest non-fortified community, a genuinely sizeable
  Métis/Catholic mission by the 1880s) but sits only ~12km from Edmonton
  itself — close enough in reality that at this board's compression it
  would land immediately hex-adjacent to Edmonton, needing a nudge large
  enough to misrepresent the real geography, and would read as "a second
  Edmonton" rather than a new place to visit. Victoria Settlement, sitting
  genuinely further out (roughly 100km east of Edmonton) gives real
  geographic spread along the corridor that St. Albert's near-Edmonton
  location does not.

**Placement:** all four project cleanly using the existing `project.mjs`
formula (no grid changes were needed to accommodate them — the northern
boreal-arc rows already had room). Fort Pitt needed no nudge; Victoria
Settlement, Fort Carlton and Batoche each needed a small (1-2 hex),
bearing-constrained nudge to clear an already-occupied hex, documented in
each entry's own `nudgeWhy` field in `cities.json`, following the same
solver methodology as the grid-rescale pass (smallest nudge along the real
compass bearing from the nearest conflicting city, not a blind search).

**Route:** the four new towns were spliced into the existing route network
in real Carlton Trail order: Edmonton – Fort Pitt – Battleford – Fort
Carlton – Batoche – Prince Albert – Regina, plus a dead-end spur from
Edmonton to Victoria Settlement (which was never on a through-route in
reality). See `docs/western-canada-board-rework-2.md` for the full build
report, including the 16-game bot measurement of how much traffic this
corridor sees now.
